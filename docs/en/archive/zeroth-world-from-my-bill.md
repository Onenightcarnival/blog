# The Zeroth World Starts With My Bill

Today I came across a phrase: the "Zeroth World." The idea is that only 10% of people — the Silicon Valley crowd — own the strongest models and resources, forming their own economy with internal growth above 50%, while everyone else is shut out of the production system.

My first reaction wasn't shock. It was recognition. Because I've already touched that wall, and it isn't in Silicon Valley. It's on my credit card statement.

## I'm Paying to Go to Work

I'm the kind of person who pays for the strongest model. I was paying back when tokens were still expensive, because it genuinely turned me into a 10x — I'm one of the first programmers to hit 10x with AI. That spending has never felt like a loss to me.

But one detail kept nagging at me: my company doesn't reimburse the subscription.

In other words, the biggest lever I use to get work done every day is something I pay for out of my own pocket. There's no line for it in the company's books. I'm paying to go to work.

It sounds absurd, but follow it through and it's actually natural. The lever of productivity has shifted from "tools the company gives you" to "compute you buy for yourself," while the employment ledger is still stuck in the old era — it still thinks it's buying my time, never realizing that the part actually doing the work is something I hired at my own expense.

## Tokens Are Valuable — You're Just Using the Wrong Ruler

Someone will say: aren't tokens getting cheaper all the time? Where's the value in that.

The price drop is real, but that's measured with the ruler of human currency. How many tokens a dollar buys does keep going up.

Swap rulers, though — how much "intelligence," how much replaced human labor a single token buys — and the conclusion flips. Models get stronger every generation, and the output you get back from the same call keeps rising. Priced in intelligence, tokens aren't depreciating. They're appreciating.

A currency holds its value through scarcity plus consensus. Tokens look deflationary — unit price trending toward zero — but that's only the nominal price. Their purchasing power, converted into intelligence, has been climbing the whole time. Change the unit of account and the whole thing stands up. This step is the crux. Skip it and everything below is castles in the air.

## There Are Exchange Rates Between Models

The interesting part isn't "models are like money." It's that exchange rates exist between models.

Claude's tokens cost more than the others, and the capability is higher too. That's like the dollar: it inflates and depreciates on its own, yet among all currencies it's still the hard one, still the most valuable. The strong model's tokens are the dollar of this ecosystem.

Go down the list — the 7B, the 30B quotas? You can be handed a fat stack of them and still not trade up to much real output. That's the Zimbabwean dollar — huge face value, tiny purchasing power.

So this isn't a single-currency world. It's a foreign exchange market. Each model is a currency, and its value depends on how much intelligence it buys. Strong models appreciate, weak ones depreciate, with a live exchange rate in between. Which currency you're holding decides your purchasing power.

## So Just Pay People in Token Quotas

For two years the industry has been cutting pay and laying people off. Push that trend to its end and the conclusion is a little cold: as labor itself gets eaten by models more and more, the very form of "salary" may stop making sense.

Why would a company pay money to buy your time? Your time's output is being replaced by models. What it actually needs to allocate is the power to call on intelligence.

So the form of compensation may change: no salary, just a token quota. How much quota of the strongest model you get is how much productivity you get, which is how much money you get.

Paying in dollars (Claude quota) versus Zimbabwean dollars (small-model quota) becomes the new salary negotiation.

## The Wall Grows Out of the Bill

Back to the Zeroth World.

It's easy to assume that wall was built deliberately by the 10% — that they hoarded the models and locked everyone else out. But from where my bill sits, it's the other way around: nobody drew that line. "Who can keep affording the top tier" sorts people into layers all by itself.

Model weights can be copied, open source will catch up eventually — all true. But access to the very top tier, and the compute quota behind it, will always be scarce and always cost money. The wall isn't in the models themselves. It's in the ability to keep paying.

And the earliest place that wall shows up isn't some grand economic structure. It's a person like me, staring at a subscription his company won't reimburse but he can't bring himself to cancel.

The Zeroth World doesn't start in Silicon Valley. It starts with my bill.
