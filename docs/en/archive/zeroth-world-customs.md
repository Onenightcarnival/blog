# Worse Than a Price You Can't Pay Is No Price at All

I'm writing this on the night of July 12. After midnight Pacific time, the model I'm writing with — Claude Fable 5 — gets pulled from my subscription and moves to metered billing: $10 per million input tokens, $50 per million output. I'm using a model with a few hours left at this price to write about why its price is going up.

Last month I wrote [The Zeroth World Starts With My Bill](./zeroth-world-from-my-bill.md). The conclusion: nobody drew the line — "who can keep paying for the strongest tier" sorted people into layers on its own. The wall grows out of the bill.

Four days after that post went out, reality issued a correction.

## June: Someone Actually Came to Draw the Line

The timeline is worth recording in full.

June 9: Fable 5 launches. June 12, after a jailbreak report from Amazon's security team, the U.S. Commerce Department issues an export-control order barring all foreign nationals from access — including Anthropic's own foreign employees. June 13: the model goes offline worldwide and refunds go out. Its public lifespan: less than 96 hours.

Two weeks later OpenAI ships GPT-5.6 and simply moves the government process up front: at the White House's request, access goes to about twenty approved organizations. No waitlist, no applications. You don't even qualify to stand in line.

Then the staged reopening: June 26, Mythos 5 opens to "trusted U.S. enterprises"; June 30, the ban is fully lifted; July 2, Fable 5 comes back worldwide; July 9, GPT-5.6 goes public. Two labs, one month, one script: cross the cyber-capability threshold, government steps in, whitelist, staged release. That's not an accident. That's the new procedure.

## Where the Permit Wall Is Worse Than the Bill Wall

In my original model, the wall was economic. Expensive, but climbable: earn more, pay more, get in. The rules were continuous. You could plan around them.

The wall that went up in June is different. It has three worse properties.

Not purchasable. For those 19 days, no amount of money got you in.

Not predictable. Anyone who bought a subscription on June 9 had no way to foresee being refunded and cut off on June 13. It's not a price curve. It's a switch.

Allocated by birth. The ban drew its line by nationality. A foreign engineer sitting in a Mountain View office couldn't access the model their own company built. The bill wall at least pretends everyone is equal. The permit wall doesn't bother.

For those 19 days I thought the last post had it wrong: the wall doesn't grow out of the bill — the government builds it.

## July: The Wall Goes Back to the Bill, Raised

The government pulled back faster than expected. Nineteen days, and the ban was gone entirely.

But look at what happened next. The reopened Fable 5 came with a clock and a cap: included for paid users through July 7, later extended to July 12, capped at half your weekly quota. After tonight, metered billing. The official line is "a temporary capacity measure — we'll restore it to subscriptions when capacity allows." No timeline.

And what came back isn't the same model. The redeployed Fable 5 runs stricter safety classifiers; trip one and your request silently downgrades to Opus 4.8. You pay Fable 5 prices and may get last generation's answers.

Lay out the whole sequence: government builds the wall, government removes it, and the market immediately takes the wall back — caps, extensions, price hikes, restraints. The administrative wall lived 19 days, because it was ugly and easy to point at. The bill wall and the classifier wall that replaced it have no expiration date, because they look like "pricing strategy" and "safety measures."

In last month's metaphor: the currency controls were lifted, but the exchange window raised its posted rate — and the teller now has the right, without telling you, to hand you last generation's currency instead of the dollars you asked for. The control didn't disappear. It turned from a decree into an exchange rate.

## The Strongest You Can Buy Isn't the Strongest That Exists

There's one character I haven't introduced yet: Mythos 5.

It's the same model as Fable 5. Same weights. One difference: Fable 5 runs three real-time safety classifiers — cybersecurity, bio and chem, distillation — that downgrade you to Opus 4.8 when tripped. Mythos 5 has them removed. What's sold on the open market is the version in shackles; the full version goes somewhere else.

Somewhere else is a list: roughly 150 organizations worldwide. Invitation-only, NDA-bound, approved by Anthropic in consultation with the U.S. government, restricted to defensive cybersecurity work. The names on it are the caliber of AWS, Apple, Google, Microsoft, NVIDIA, JPMorgan. No applications. No waitlist.

GPT-5.6's government gate came down after 13 days — you could still call that temporary. Mythos's gate has been there since launch day, June 9. The controls came, it was there; the controls went, it's still there. It isn't a product of the crisis. It's the standing structure.

This is the heaviest correction to last month's post. My assumption was that paying for the strongest tier buys you into the zeroth world — money is the ticket. Expensive, but posted. Mythos says the assumption fails at the root: the ceiling of the open market is designed to sit below the real ceiling, and what separates them isn't a price. It's an invitation. The inner circle of the zeroth world isn't "10% of people." It's a specific, finite, countable list.

The employee I pay for out of my own pocket has a twin who works without the shackles, inside the networks of 150 organizations. I don't even get to see his rate card.

## "Can't Afford It" Is the Gentler Kind of Rejection

So was last month's post wrong? It was — wrong in the optimistic direction.

In that post's world, the zeroth world at its worst was still a market: the wall was high, but the price was posted. Not affording it was my problem, and in theory more income could solve it. That was the bill wall's one mercy — it left a path. Steep, but real.

This month I walked that path to its end. The part money can solve stops at Fable 5: the caps, the extensions, the metered billing are all price problems — expensive, but computable. Above that, the price dimension simply ends. Nineteen days of export controls that no money could cross; a list of 150 organizations that takes no applications. The ceiling I used to see was only the outer ring's ceiling.

The outer wall still rises by ratchet: every crisis adds a course of bricks, and when the crisis passes, the bricks stay. Between "included in your subscription" and "$50 per million output tokens, billed separately" sits one national-security incident. But the inner wall needs no ratchet. It was welded shut on June 9.

Last month my worry was that one day I couldn't pay. Now I know: "can't afford it" is the gentler kind of rejection the zeroth world offers — at least it still counts you as a potential customer.

This post goes out before midnight. Starting tomorrow, the next one costs a different price. At least there's still a price.
